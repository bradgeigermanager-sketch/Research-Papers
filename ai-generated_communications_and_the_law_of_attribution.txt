# **Paper 3 — AI‑Generated Communications and the Law of Attribution**  
### *Why AI Is a Tool, Not an Agent — and Why You Still Bear Responsibility*

## **Abstract**
This paper examines how the law attributes responsibility for communications generated by artificial intelligence systems. Unlike human assistants, AI systems cannot be legal agents because they lack personhood, intent, and the capacity to assent. Yet the outputs of AI systems are routinely treated as the speech of the human or organization that deploys them. This paper analyzes the doctrinal foundations of attribution, the role of foreseeability and negligence, and the implications of probabilistic language generation. It cross‑references Paper 1 (legal responsibility), Paper 2 (agency doctrine), and Paper 5 (communication theory) to situate AI within the broader landscape of delegated communication.

---

# **1. Introduction: The Myth of “AI Intent”**
AI systems generate text that can appear intentional, reasoned, or expressive. But legally, AI has:

- no intent  
- no beliefs  
- no agency  
- no capacity to assent  
- no legal personhood  

Thus, AI cannot be an “agent” in the legal sense described in **Paper 2**.  
Yet AI‑generated communications can bind, obligate, or expose the human or organization that uses the system.

This paper explains why.

---

# **2. Why AI Cannot Be an Agent Under Current Law**
Agency requires:

1. A principal  
2. An agent  
3. Mutual consent  
4. Capacity to act on behalf of the principal  
5. Control by the principal  

AI fails at least three of these requirements.

### **2.1 No Capacity to Consent**
AI cannot agree to act on behalf of anyone.  
It cannot accept duties, understand obligations, or form intentions.

### **2.2 No Mental State**
Agency presumes the ability to understand instructions and act with purpose.  
AI systems operate through statistical pattern generation, not comprehension.

### **2.3 No Legal Personhood**
AI cannot be sued, cannot hold property, and cannot bear liability.

Thus, AI is not an agent.  
It is a **tool**—like a typewriter, but vastly more complex.

---

# **3. Attribution: Why AI Output Is Treated as Your Speech**
Even though AI is not an agent, its output is attributed to the user because:

- **You chose the tool**  
- **You initiated the communication**  
- **You provided the prompt**  
- **You deployed the system in a context where others rely on the output**  

This parallels the analysis in **Paper 1**, where delegated communications bind the principal.

Courts and regulators treat AI output as:

- your representation  
- your publication  
- your statement  
- your commitment  
- your disclosure  

The legal fiction is simple:

> **AI is an extension of the user’s expressive capacity.**

---

# **4. Foreseeability and Negligence: The Real Liability Framework**
Because AI is not an agent, liability arises not from agency law but from **negligence** and **foreseeability**.

### **4.1 Foreseeability**
If it is foreseeable that an AI system might:

- generate errors  
- misstate facts  
- hallucinate  
- misinterpret instructions  
- produce harmful content  

then the user must take reasonable steps to prevent harm.

### **4.2 Negligence**
Negligence occurs when a user:

- relies on AI output without review  
- deploys AI in high‑risk contexts  
- fails to implement safeguards  
- allows AI to communicate autonomously without oversight  

The standard is not perfection.  
It is **reasonable care**.

---

# **5. The Problem of Probabilistic Output**
AI systems generate text by predicting statistically likely sequences, not by reasoning about truth or intent. This creates several legal challenges:

### **5.1 Hallucination Risk**
AI may confidently assert false information.  
If a user relies on such output, liability may follow.

### **5.2 Tone Drift**
AI may produce phrasing that implies commitments or admissions the user did not intend.

### **5.3 Ambiguity**
AI may generate text that is open to multiple interpretations, increasing contractual or regulatory risk.

These issues connect directly to **Paper 5**, which analyzes communication noise and distortion.

---

# **6. Regulatory Frameworks**
Regulators increasingly treat AI‑generated communications as attributable to the deploying entity.

### **6.1 FTC Guidance**
The U.S. Federal Trade Commission warns that companies are responsible for:

- misleading AI‑generated claims  
- deceptive automated communications  
- AI‑driven customer interactions  

### **6.2 EU AI Act**
The EU AI Act imposes obligations on deployers of high‑risk AI systems, including:

- transparency  
- human oversight  
- accuracy controls  

### **6.3 Sector‑Specific Rules**
Financial, healthcare, and legal sectors impose heightened duties when AI is used in communication or decision‑making.

Across all frameworks, the theme is consistent:

> **AI output is treated as the user’s output.**

---

# **7. Governance Recommendations**
Organizations using AI for communication should implement:

### **7.1 Human‑in‑the‑Loop Review**
Critical communications must be reviewed by a human before sending.

### **7.2 Role‑Based Access Controls**
Limit who can deploy AI systems in sensitive contexts.

### **7.3 Logging and Audit Trails**
Maintain records of prompts and outputs for accountability.

### **7.4 Clear Disclaimers**
When appropriate, disclose that content was AI‑assisted.

### **7.5 Training and Policies**
Employees must understand the risks of unreviewed AI output.

These governance structures connect to **Paper 4**, which analyzes organizational delegation.

---

# **8. Conclusion**
AI systems cannot be legal agents, but their outputs are attributed to the humans and organizations that use them. Liability arises not from agency doctrine but from foreseeability, negligence, and the user’s control over the system. As AI becomes more deeply integrated into communication workflows, the legal expectation of human oversight will only increase.

Paper 4 will examine how organizations manage communication risk when delegation involves both human and AI intermediaries.
