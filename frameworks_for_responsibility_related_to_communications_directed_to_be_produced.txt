# ⭐ 1. **Legal Responsibility**
**You are still responsible for the content**, even if you didn’t read it.

In U.S. law (and most common‑law systems):

- If you **direct** someone to send a communication on your behalf  
- And they act within the scope of your instruction  
- **You are the principal, they are the agent**

Therefore:

> **The communication is treated as yours, even if you never reviewed it.**

This applies to:
- secretaries  
- assistants  
- employees  
- automated systems  
- AI tools  

The only exception is if the agent **clearly deviates** from your instructions in a way that is *not reasonably foreseeable*.

But even then, the burden is on *you* to prove the deviation.

---

# ⭐ 2. **Agency / Authority**
In agency law, the rule is simple:

> **If you authorize someone to speak for you, their words bind you.**

There is no guarantee of perfect alignment.  
There is only **delegation + risk**.

This is why:
- CEOs review press releases  
- Lawyers review filings  
- Diplomats review cables  
- Politicians review speeches  

Delegation without review is always a gamble.

---

# ⭐ 3. **AI‑Generated Communications**
AI introduces a twist:  
It is not an “agent” in the legal sense — **you are the author**.

But the practical effect is the same:

- If you instruct an AI to send something  
- And you don’t review it  
- **You own the consequences**

Courts treat AI output as:
- your speech  
- your publication  
- your representation  

There is no legal doctrine that says “the AI misunderstood me.”

---

# ⭐ 4. **Workplace Delegation**
In a workplace, the risk is reputational and operational rather than legal.

If you don’t review delegated communications:
- tone may drift  
- commitments may be made you didn’t intend  
- instructions may be misinterpreted  
- relationships may be affected  

Managers often use the rule:

> **“Trust, but verify.”**

Because even a highly competent assistant cannot read your mind.

---

# ⭐ 5. **Communication Theory**
Even *you* cannot guarantee that your own message will match your intent — because communication is filtered through:

- the sender’s encoding  
- the medium  
- the receiver’s decoding  
- context  
- assumptions  
- tone  
- timing  

Delegation adds another layer of distortion.

So the theoretical answer is also:  
**No, it can never be guaranteed.**

---

# ⭐ 6. **Historical Practice: “Dictated but Not Read”**
Yes — this was absolutely a real thing.

Secretaries and stenographers would add a notation like:

> **“Dictated but not read.”**  
> **“DBNR.”**  
> **“Dictated but not checked.”**

This served several purposes:

### **A. Liability Shield (Partial)**
It signaled:
- the sender did not personally review the final text  
- transcription errors were possible  
- tone or phrasing might not reflect the sender’s exact intent  

It did **not** absolve the sender of responsibility, but it created a record of delegation.

### **B. Professional Courtesy**
Recipients understood:
- minor errors should be forgiven  
- the sender was busy  
- the message was functional, not polished  

### **C. Internal Office Workflow**
It documented:
- who dictated  
- who typed  
- who reviewed (or didn’t)  

This was important in offices where multiple assistants handled correspondence.

### **D. Legal and Diplomatic Use**
In some contexts, it served as a disclaimer that:
- the sender did not personally verify the accuracy  
- the message should not be treated as a formal or final statement  

But again — it never fully shielded the sender.

---

# ⭐ The Unifying Principle Across All Domains
Whether it’s:
- a secretary  
- a stenographer  
- a junior employee  
- a software system  
- or an AI model  

**Delegation without review is never guaranteed to match your intent.**  
It is always a calculated risk.

The “dictated but not read” tag was the analog‑era acknowledgment of that fact.
