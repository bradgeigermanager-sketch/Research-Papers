This portrayal is ‌a well-documented, highly effective tactic that bad actors actively use to conceal their advanced technology/knowledge, create blind spots, and hamper the efforts of the teams or organizations they are embedded within or observing‌. It works by weaponizing the target's own assumptions about technical skill, and it operates through multiple layered, low-detection pathways.

Core Concealment Mechanisms Bad Actors Use
Lowering defensive vigilance‌: By performing as technically unskilled, they avoid being flagged as high-risk targets. Teams will skip targeted monitoring, technical access restrictions, or deep behavioral audits for someone they perceive as a "non-threat," giving the bad actor free rein to quietly operate advanced tools without triggering alerts .
Normalizing hidden advanced actions‌: They can frame any accidental or minor technical misstep as the work of a clumsy novice, while their actual sophisticated operations (like using LOLBins for lateral movement, or hiding malicious code on 404 error pages) get written off as routine, low-skill user errors. This makes their real advanced activity nearly indistinguishable from normal, low-stakes user behavior .
Avoiding mandatory knowledge disclosure‌: They can plausibly claim they "don't understand the tech well enough" to contribute to technical discussions, so no one will press them to explain their work, share their processes, or demonstrate their actual technical capabilities. This lets them keep their full expertise and hidden advanced tools completely unexamined.
How It Directly Hampers Local/Observing Efforts
It creates a false "fog of misperception" that distorts the entire team's threat assessment. The group will systematically underestimate the adversary's capacity, leading them to deprioritize critical defensive upgrades, skip threat-hunting drills, and ignore obvious anomalous behavior that would otherwise be flagged .
It lets the bad actor embed themselves as a "harmless, low-skill participant" for extremely long periods. Modern APT and ransomware groups already use this exact "blending in" tactic, mimicking normal, low-privilege user behavior to extend their dwell time inside target environments by months or even years, while quietly exfiltrating data or sabotaging systems from within .
It poisons collective situational awareness: When the entire team has bought into the narrative that this actor is technically incompetent, any warning signs or red flags about their advanced activity will be dismissed out of hand, making coordinated, effective response nearly impossible until significant damage is already done.

This is not a theoretical risk — it is a classic social engineering + operational security tactic that overlaps heavily with the "low and slow" concealment strategies modern malicious cyber groups already rely on to evade even mature defensive systems.
