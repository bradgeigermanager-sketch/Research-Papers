### How the collapse of predictive exclusivity maps onto CIs/HUMINT

Let’s anchor that lemma directly into your Informant–Handler Game and the CI/HUMINT world you’re modeling.

---

#### 1. No one actor owns future risk

In CI/HUMINT environments:

- **Handlers** form expectations about:
  - whether a source will deliver,  
  - whether a target will act,  
  - whether a threat will materialize.

- **Sources (CIs/CHIS)** form expectations about:
  - how much protection they’ll get,  
  - how much they can exploit the relationship,  
  - how likely retaliation or exposure is.

- **Other actors**—rival agencies, supervisors, prosecutors, offenders, media, foreign services—also form expectations about:
  - future enforcement intensity,  
  - future surveillance,  
  - future risk of arrest,  
  - future political or public reaction.

Your lemma says:  
no single actor controls that expectation field.

Even if a handler believes “my forecast of risk is the one that matters,” other actors are simultaneously:

- pricing risk differently,  
- planning around different futures,  
- and acting on those divergent expectations.

---

#### 2. Strategic informants and expectation competition

For CIs/HUMINT, this has teeth:

- A CI may try to **shape the handler’s expectations** (“this gang is about to escalate”, “this target is low‑risk”).  
- But other sources, technical collection, rival agencies, and even offenders’ own behavior are **simultaneously shaping expectations** elsewhere.

So:

- The handler’s forecast \(\Pi_H(P)\) is **not** the only forecast.  
- The CI’s internal forecast \(\Pi_S(P)\) is **not** the only forecast.  
- Offenders’ forecasts \(\Pi_O(P)\) (about enforcement, betrayal, retaliation) are **not** controlled by the handler–CI dyad.

Your lemma \(\ref{lemma:prediction}\) is basically saying:

> In a multi‑actor CI/HUMINT environment, no one can monopolize how the future is mentally priced in.

---

#### 3. Consequences for your Informant–Handler Game

In your game:

- The handler’s strategy depends on **expected future utility** of trusting or distrusting the CI.  
- The CI’s strategy depends on **expected future utility** of fabricating, shading, or telling the truth.

But:

- Supervisors may expect different payoffs from the same case.  
- Other agencies may expect different threat trajectories.  
- Offenders may expect different enforcement patterns and adjust behavior.  
- Media/public may expect different scandal probabilities.

So the “future” is not a single object—it’s a **contested expectation space**.

That means:

- A CI cannot safely assume that “if I make the handler believe X about the future, that’s the future that matters.”  
- A handler cannot safely assume that “my risk forecast is the one that shapes behavior.”

Your lemma formalizes that:

\[
\Pi_{X_f}(P) \text{ is never the unique or expectation-dominant prediction of } P
\]

once multiple actors with different information and incentives are in play.

---

#### 4. Why this matters for fabrication and distortion

This ties directly back to your core theme—fabricated intelligence and distorted incentives:

- If a CI fabricates to **shape expectations** (e.g., exaggerating threat to secure protection or resources), that fabrication enters a **multi‑actor expectation field**.  
- Other actors’ forecasts may contradict, dilute, or override it.  
- The CI’s manipulation of one handler’s expectations does not guarantee control over:
  - prosecutorial expectations,  
  - offender expectations,  
  - media expectations,  
  - other agencies’ expectations.

So the rational CI, in your model, must recognize:

- **Expectation manipulation is not exclusive.**  
- Other actors are simultaneously predicting and planning.  
- Fabrications that rely on owning the future narrative are structurally fragile.

And the rational handler must recognize:

- **Risk perception is not centrally controlled.**  
- Their forecast is one of many.  
- Overconfidence in their own expectations is itself a vulnerability.
