## Research Paper: The Transfusion of Conflicts Law into Criminal Procedure

**Abstract**

Historically, the fields of Conflict of Laws (private international law) and criminal procedure have functioned as distinct and isolated domains of American jurisprudence. Conflicts of laws—concerned primarily with civil litigation, contracts, and torts—have developed a sophisticated, albeit fragmented, framework for reconciling disuniformity between jurisdictions. Conversely, criminal procedure has long relied upon a territorial "floor" of constitutional protections, largely ignoring the choice-of-law dilemmas that arise when criminal acts or investigations bridge sovereign boundaries. As modern policing and digital-age criminality increase the frequency of multi-jurisdictional entanglement, this paper argues for a formal "transfusion" of conflicts law into criminal procedure. It explores the inadequacies of the current territorial approach and proposes a modernized choice-of-law framework centered on the principle of the "most significant relationship."

---

### I. Introduction: The Collision of Two Distant Cousins

For decades, the "Conflict of Laws" has been treated as a specialized niche for civil litigators—a domain of *lex loci delicti* and interest analysis. Criminal procedure, meanwhile, has been defined by the constitutional floor established by the U.S. Supreme Court, ensuring that regardless of geography, certain minimum rights prevail. However, the rise of digital evidence, cross-border investigations, and shifting state standards regarding privacy and interrogation has created a "disuniformity gap." When a suspect is interrogated in State A under rules that would be inadmissible in State B, but the evidence is intended for use in a State B prosecution, the traditional "forum law" approach of criminal procedure fails to address the underlying normative conflict.

### II. The Territorial Limitation and the Rise of Disuniformity

The traditional American approach to criminal jurisdiction is tethered to the "situs rule"—the law of the place where the crime occurred. While simple, this rule is increasingly brittle.

1. **The Erosion of Territoriality:** In cases of cybercrime or multi-state fraud, the "situs" of the crime is often a legal fiction. If a defendant in State X hacks a server in State Y, the "location" of the wrong is simultaneously everywhere and nowhere.
2. **State-Level Rights Inflation:** The Supreme Court sets the *minimum* constitutional floor, but states are increasingly expanding protections (e.g., more rigorous requirements for recording interrogations or higher standards for electronic surveillance). This creates a situation where a piece of evidence is "legal" in the jurisdiction of collection but "unconstitutional" by the standards of the forum jurisdiction.

### III. Conflicts Principles as a Remedial Framework

The application of civil conflict-of-laws principles to criminal matters offers a more nuanced methodology than the binary "admissible/inadmissible" approach.

#### A. Interest Analysis

Under the "interest analysis" popularized by Brainerd Currie, courts identify the state with the greatest interest in having its law applied. In a criminal context, this involves weighing the interests of the *investigating state* (which has a sovereign interest in the integrity of its police procedures) against the interests of the *prosecuting state* (which has an interest in the trial's fairness to the defendant).

#### B. The "Most Significant Relationship" Test

The Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws proposes that the law of the state with the "most significant relationship" to the occurrence should govern. Applying this to criminal procedure would require courts to analyze:

* The domicile of the defendant.
* The location of the primary investigative act.
* The nexus between the evidence collected and the forum's public policy regarding rights.

### IV. Challenges and Critiques

Critics of integrating conflicts principles into criminal law often cite two major concerns:

1. **Public Policy (Ordre Public):** A state has a strong, non-waivable interest in maintaining its own standards of criminal justice. Allowing "foreign" procedural law to govern a local prosecution could be seen as an affront to the forum's sovereignty.
2. **The Constitutional Floor:** Critics argue that the Fourth and Fifth Amendments should be interpreted uniformly by federal courts, thereby rendering choice-of-law analysis unnecessary. However, as the Supreme Court continues to signal an intent to return authority to the states, the gap in procedural protections will likely widen, making a conflicts-based approach more, not less, necessary.

### V. Conclusion: Toward a Harmonized Doctrine

Criminal procedure is no longer an insular, purely local endeavor. As procedural protections vary between sovereign states, the legal system must adopt a mechanism for resolving these clashes that does not rely on outdated territorial dogmas. By importing the "most significant relationship" test and refining interest analysis for the criminal context, courts can ensure that the administration of justice remains consistent, predictable, and fundamentally fair in an increasingly interconnected legal landscape.

### Procedure: Analyzing Jurisdiction in Multi-Jurisdictional Criminal Matters

If you are tasked with identifying potential jurisdictional conflicts in a legal or research project, use the following procedure:

1. **Inventory Jurisdictional Links:** Map the event against all involved states.
* *Where did the investigation occur?*
* *Where were the primary effects of the crime felt?*
* *Where is the defendant domiciled?*


2. **Identify Procedural Disparity:** Compare the rules of evidence and suspect protection in each involved jurisdiction.
* *Note "slots" for specific data:* [List the specific rule in question: e.g., Warrantless search standards].


3. **Perform Interest Analysis:**
* Which state has the greatest sovereign interest in the legality of the *collection* of evidence?
* Which state has the greatest interest in the *adjudication* of the trial?


4. **Evaluate Public Policy (Ordre Public) Exceptions:** Does the application of the "foreign" law violate a fundamental, deeply held policy of the forum court?
5. **Drafting/Argument Phase:** Apply the "most significant relationship" test to justify why the law of Jurisdiction A should prevail over Jurisdiction B.
