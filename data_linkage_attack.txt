Dataset linkage attacks happen when an attacker takes **two or more datasets that look harmless on their own** and combines them to reveal identities or sensitive facts. It’s one of the most common ways “anonymized” data gets broken.

Below is a clear, structured explanation with examples, visuals, and Guided Links so you can explore any part more deeply.

---

## 🔍 What a dataset linkage attack is  
A dataset linkage attack occurs when someone links **quasi‑identifiers** (indirect clues) across datasets to re‑identify individuals.

Examples of quasi‑identifiers:  
- ZIP code  
- Birthdate  
- Gender  
- Location + time  
- Movie ratings  
- Search queries  

These aren’t private by themselves — but together, they become a fingerprint.

---

## 🧩 How linkage attacks work  
Here’s the basic process:

1. **Find overlapping attributes**  
   The attacker looks for fields that appear in both datasets (e.g., ZIP code + age).

2. **Cross‑match records**  
   They match rows from one dataset to rows in another using those shared attributes.

3. **Narrow down uniqueness**  
   If only one person fits the combination, the identity is revealed.

4. **Extract sensitive info**  
   Once the identity is known, all associated data becomes exposed.

---

## 📌 Real‑world examples of linkage attacks  
These are some of the most famous cases where linkage broke “anonymous” data.

### 1. **Netflix Prize + IMDb**  
Netflix released anonymized movie ratings. IMDb has public ratings.  
By linking rating patterns, researchers identified specific Netflix users.

Why it worked:  
Movie rating patterns are unique — like a fingerprint.

---

### 2. **Massachusetts Hospital Data + Voter Rolls**  
The state released anonymized hospital records.  
Voter rolls contain ZIP code, birthdate, and gender.

Linking the two datasets revealed the governor’s medical records.

Why it worked:  
87% of Americans are uniquely identifiable by ZIP + birthdate + gender.

---

### 3. **NYC Taxi Logs + Public Photos**  
NYC released taxi trip data.  
Paparazzi photos show celebrities entering taxis at specific times.

Linking timestamps exposed celebrities’ trips and tips.

Why it worked:  
Time + location is extremely identifying.

---

### 4. **Strava Heatmap + Satellite Imagery**  
Strava published global fitness heatmaps.  
Satellite imagery shows building layouts.

Linking the two revealed secret military bases and patrol routes.

Why it worked:  
Sparse data in remote areas becomes highly identifying.

---

## 🧠 Why linkage attacks are so powerful  
Even if each dataset is “safe,” combining them creates a **mosaic effect** — small pieces form a complete picture.





Key reasons linkage works:  
- Humans have unique behavior patterns  
- Geography and time are extremely identifying  
- Public datasets are everywhere (voter rolls, social media, business records)  
- Attackers only need a few overlapping clues  

---

## 🔐 What makes a dataset vulnerable  
A dataset is at high risk of linkage if it contains:

- **Quasi‑identifiers** (ZIP, age, gender)  
- **Location data** (GPS, neighborhoods, routes)  
- **Temporal data** (timestamps, sequences)  
- **Behavioral patterns** (ratings, purchases, searches)  
- **Sparse categories** (rare diseases, small towns)  

Even if names are removed, these patterns can identify someone.

---

## 🛡️ How to defend against linkage attacks  
Organizations use techniques like:

- **k‑anonymity**  
- **l‑diversity**  
- **Differential privacy**  
- **Generalization**  
- **Noise injection**  

Each method reduces the uniqueness of records so they can’t be linked.

### Scenario setup

- **Dataset A (released “anonymized”)**: Hospital records  
  - Fields: ZIP code, birthdate, gender, diagnosis, visit dates  
  - Names and direct identifiers removed  

- **Dataset B (public)**: Voter registration records  
  - Fields: Name, address, ZIP code, birthdate, gender  

The goal of the attacker: connect “anonymous” hospital records to real people.

---

### Step 1: Collect candidate datasets

**Action:**  
The attacker finds two datasets that likely describe the same population or overlap heavily (e.g., residents of the same state or city).

- Hospital discharge data (de‑identified but detailed)   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12844594/)  
- Public voter rolls or other registries with names and demographics   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3638181/)  

**Why this matters:**  
Linkage only works if there are shared attributes across datasets.

---

### Step 2: Identify quasi‑identifiers

**Action:**  
The attacker scans both datasets to find fields that appear in both:

- ZIP code  
- Birthdate  
- Gender  

These are **quasi‑identifiers**—not secret by themselves, but powerful in combination.   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12844594/)  

**Why this matters:**  
These shared fields become the “keys” for matching records.

---

### Step 3: Standardize and clean the data

**Action:**  
The attacker normalizes formats so they line up:

- Convert dates to the same format (e.g., YYYY‑MM‑DD)  
- Normalize ZIP codes (5‑digit vs 9‑digit)  
- Ensure gender codes match (M/F vs 0/1)  

**Why this matters:**  
Small formatting differences can prevent matches; cleaning maximizes linkage success.

---

### Step 4: Generate candidate matches

**Action:**  
For each hospital record, the attacker searches the voter roll for people with the **same**:

- ZIP code  
- Birthdate  
- Gender  

This can be done with simple database joins or scripts—no fancy AI required.   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3638181/)  

**Why this matters:**  
Now each “anonymous” hospital row has a small set of possible real‑world identities.

---

### Step 5: Exploit uniqueness

**Action:**  
The attacker looks for **unique combinations**:

- If only **one** voter in that ZIP has that exact birthdate and gender, the match is effectively certain.  
- If there are a few candidates, the attacker may still narrow it down using extra fields (e.g., race, party, or city).   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12844594/)  

**Why this matters:**  
Research has shown that a large fraction of people are uniquely identifiable by ZIP + birthdate + gender alone.

---

### Step 6: Attach sensitive attributes

**Action:**  
Once a hospital record is linked to a specific person from the voter roll:

- The attacker now knows that **this named person** had **this diagnosis**, **this procedure**, or **this visit history**.  
- Sensitive medical details—originally “anonymous”—are now tied to a real identity.   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3638181/)  

**Why this matters:**  
The privacy of the entire “anonymized” dataset is undermined, not just one person.

---

### Step 7: Scale up and refine

**Action:**  
The attacker automates the process:

- Runs linkage across all records  
- Uses probabilistic matching for near‑matches (e.g., small date errors)  
- Validates a subset manually to estimate accuracy  

**Why this matters:**  
Even if only a fraction of records are confidently re‑identified, that can be enough to cause serious privacy harm and demonstrate that the anonymization is broken.   [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11717198/)  [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12844594/)  

---

### The core insight

A linkage attack is basically this:

> “Find overlapping clues across datasets, then let uniqueness do the work.”
