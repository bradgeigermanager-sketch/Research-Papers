### Interpretation lemma for the Informant–Handler Game

Here’s the matching lemma, parallel to the observation/response lemmas, ready to drop into your LaTeX model.

```latex
\begin{lemma}[Interpretive Multiplicity in Multi-Actor Environments]
\label{lemma:interpretation}
Let an event or problem $P$ be observed by a set of observers
$\mathcal{O} = \{O_1, \dots, O_n\}$, each with its own information set
$I_i$ and interpretive map
\[
N_i = f_i(P, I_i, \theta_i),
\]
where $\theta_i$ encodes observer $O_i$'s priors, incentives, and
institutional constraints.

Suppose a focal actor $O_f \in \mathcal{O}$ believes it can monopolize
the interpretation of $P$ (``interpretive exclusivity''). If there
exists at least one additional observer $O_k \neq O_f$ such that
\[
\Pr(O_k \text{ observes } P) > 0
\quad\text{and}\quad
f_k \neq f_f,
\]
then the assumption of interpretive exclusivity by $O_f$ is
structurally unstable.

As the number of observers $|\mathcal{O}|$ with heterogeneous
$(I_i,\theta_i)$ increases, the probability that $N_f$ remains the sole
or uncontested interpretation of $P$ converges to zero, and the expected
payoff to any strategy that relies on uncontested narrative control
strictly decreases.

Consequently, in any multi-actor environment with overlapping
observation and heterogeneous interpretive maps, equilibrium strategies
must treat meaning as plural and contested rather than singular and
centrally controlled.
\end{lemma}
```

### Interpretation Graph: extended description

This is the third diagram in the trilogy, layered on top of the Observer Graph and Response Space.

#### 1. Core idea

- The **Observer Graph** says: many see.  
- The **Response Graph** says: many act.  
- The **Interpretation Graph** says: many *mean*.

The Interpretation Graph is a network of **narrative nodes** and their relationships, generated by observers looking at the same event.

---

#### 2. Node types

1. **Event node \(P\)**  
   - Same central event as in the Observer Graph.

2. **Observer nodes \(O_i\)**  
   - As before: state, criminal, private, media/public, automated.

3. **Interpretation nodes \(N_i\)**  
   - One (or more) per observer.  
   - Each \(N_i\) is the narrative, frame, or analytic product produced by \(O_i\).  
   - Examples:
     - “Legitimate law enforcement operation”  
     - “State repression”  
     - “Business opportunity”  
     - “Platform policy violation”  
     - “Moral outrage”  
     - “Statistical anomaly”

4. **Meta‑interpretation nodes \(M_j\)** (optional)  
   - Higher‑order interpretations (e.g., pundits, analysts, academics) that interpret *other interpretations*.

---

#### 3. Edge types

1. **Observation edges**  
   - \(P \rightarrow O_i\) (from Observer Graph).

2. **Interpretation edges**  
   - \(O_i \rightarrow N_i\): observer \(O_i\) generates narrative \(N_i\).

3. **Conflict / alignment edges between interpretations**  
   - \(N_i \leftrightarrow N_j\) with labels:
     - “reinforces”  
     - “contradicts”  
     - “competes with”  
     - “co‑opts”  
   - You can encode:
     - solid line = reinforcement  
     - dashed line = competition  
     - red line = contradiction

4. **Propagation edges**  
   - \(N_i \rightarrow O_j\): observer \(O_j\) adopts or reacts to narrative \(N_i\).  
   - \(N_i \rightarrow M_k\): meta‑interpreters consuming narratives.

5. **Aggregation edges** (optional)  
   - Multiple \(N_i\) feeding into a “dominant narrative” node \(D\), representing temporary consensus or hegemonic framing.

---

#### 4. Structural patterns

1. **Narrative clusters**  
   - Interpretations that are mutually reinforcing form clusters:
     - e.g., several state actors + some media outlets sharing a similar frame.

2. **Narrative fault lines**  
   - Dense internal agreement within clusters, sharp disagreement across clusters:
     - e.g., state vs criminal vs activist interpretations.

3. **Hegemonic narrative node \(D\)** (optional)  
   - A node representing the currently dominant public meaning.  
   - Edges from \(N_i\) to \(D\) weighted by reach/influence.

4. **Fringe narratives**  
   - Isolated \(N_i\) with few edges; low adoption but potentially high volatility if they suddenly propagate.

---

#### 5. Dynamics

1. **Update process**  
   - As new information arrives, \(I_i\) changes → \(N_i\) may update.  
   - Edges \(N_i \leftrightarrow N_j\) can change type (from contradiction to partial alignment, etc.).

2. **Contestation**  
   - Multiple \(N_i\) compete for influence over \(D\).  
   - External shocks (new evidence, leaks, events) can flip which cluster dominates.

3. **Adversarial injection**  
   - New \(N_k\) introduced by adversaries to:
     - fragment consensus,  
     - discredit other narratives,  
     - or hijack the dominant frame.

4. **Lock‑in**  
   - Once \(D\) stabilizes, some \(N_i\) may align with it even if their original \(f_i\) would have produced something else (conformity, institutional pressure).

---

#### 6. TikZ layout sketch (conceptual)

You can implement something like:

- Place \(P\) at center.  
- Place \(O_i\) on an inner ring.  
- Place \(N_i\) on an outer ring, each connected to its \(O_i\).  
- Draw edges between \(N_i\) to show reinforcement/contradiction.  
- Optionally, place a dominant narrative node \(D\) above or below, with incoming edges from \(N_i\).
