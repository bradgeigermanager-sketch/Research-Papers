Attacks inducing deauthentication to enable malicious code insertion or other system or network changes in the absence of the authorized users or devices

**Scenario Overview**

1. **Attacker Forces Deauthentication**

   * Target: User devices, IoT devices, or systems connected to a network.
   * Method: Sending deauthentication frames (for Wi-Fi), session termination commands, or disconnecting users from a service.
   * Result: Device temporarily loses connectivity or loses its authenticated session.

2. **Vulnerability Window**

   * During or immediately after deauthentication:

     * Devices may **automatically try to reconnect**.
     * Devices may **pull updates, configurations, or policies** once reconnected.
     * The system might **accept instructions from any source that appears legitimate**.

3. **Exploitation**

   * Attacker uses the window created by deauthentication to:

     * **Insert new instructions** (e.g., malicious scripts, rogue policies).
     * **Substitute or replace existing instructions** (e.g., replace legitimate firmware with compromised firmware).
     * **Manipulate configuration data** (e.g., DNS settings, routing tables, or security policies).
   * Techniques can include:

     * **Evil Twin / Rogue AP**: Device connects to a fake network controlled by the attacker.
     * **Session Hijacking / MITM**: Attacker intercepts requests during reconnection.
     * **Update or Policy Injection**: Target fetches instructions from a compromised server or attacker-supplied resource.

4. **Outcome**

   * Device executes malicious instructions believing they are legitimate.
   * Data, policies, or configurations can be silently modified or replaced.
   * The attack may persist even after reconnection to the legitimate network, if the instructions remain active.

---

### **Key Characteristics**

* **Deauthentication is a tool, not the end goal**: It creates a window of opportunity.
* **Timing is critical**: The attacker exploits the reconnection or resynchronization process.
* **Trust exploitation**: Systems often assume that instructions or policies fetched post-reconnection are trustworthy.
* **Can target multiple layers**: Network-level (Wi-Fi), application-level (cloud services), or device-level (IoT firmware).

---

### **Examples in Practice**

1. **Wi-Fi Networks**

   * Attacker sends deauth frames → device reconnects to rogue AP → rogue AP supplies malicious DHCP, DNS, or configuration instructions.

2. **IoT Device Exploitation**

   * Smart home device is disconnected → reconnects to compromised update server → attacker substitutes firmware or automation rules.

3. **Enterprise Systems**

   * VPN or remote desktop session is terminated → user reconnects → attacker injects malicious group policies or scripts via a compromised update endpoint.

---

### **Defensive Strategies**

* **Mutual authentication for all instructions and updates**
* **Digital signatures and integrity checks** for all policies, configurations, and firmware
* **Alerting on unusual deauthentication patterns**
* **Network segmentation** to reduce what instructions can be changed if a device is compromised
* **Rate limiting and monitoring deauthentication attempts** to prevent forced disconnects
