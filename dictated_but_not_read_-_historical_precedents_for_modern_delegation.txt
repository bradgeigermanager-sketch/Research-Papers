# **Paper 6 — “Dictated But Not Read”: Historical Precedents for Modern Delegation**  
### *How Stenographers, Secretaries, and Early Office Systems Managed Unreviewed Messages*

## **Abstract**
This paper examines the historical practice of “Dictated but not read” (DBNR) and related notations used in business, legal, and governmental correspondence from the late 19th through the 20th century. These practices served as early acknowledgments of the risks inherent in delegated communication—risks that persist today in AI‑assisted drafting and automated messaging systems. By analyzing DBNR as a socio‑technical artifact, the paper connects historical delegation workflows to the legal doctrines explored in Papers 1 and 2, the attribution issues in Paper 3, the organizational dynamics in Paper 4, and the communication‑theory limits described in Paper 5.

---

# **1. Introduction: The Forgotten Precursor to AI‑Assisted Communication**
Long before email, CRM systems, or AI models, organizations relied on human intermediaries—stenographers, typists, secretaries—to produce written communication. These intermediaries often drafted messages based on oral dictation, shorthand notes, or incomplete instructions.

To signal that the principal had not personally reviewed the final text, secretaries added a notation:

> **“Dictated but not read.”**  
> **“DBNR.”**  
> **“Dictated but not checked.”**

This simple phrase captured a profound truth:

> **Delegated communication carries inherent risk.**

This paper explores how DBNR functioned as a historical risk‑management tool and how its logic persists in modern communication systems.

---

# **2. Origins of the DBNR Notation**
The DBNR notation emerged in the late 1800s as stenography and typewriting became widespread in business and government.

### **2.1 The Rise of Stenographers**
Stenographers captured dictation in shorthand and later transcribed it into typed letters. Errors were common due to:
- misheard words  
- ambiguous phrasing  
- incomplete dictation  
- transcription mistakes  

### **2.2 The Typewriter Revolution**
Typed letters became the professional standard, but principals rarely typed their own correspondence. Delegation became the norm.

### **2.3 The Need for a Disclaimer**
DBNR emerged as a way to:
- acknowledge the delegation  
- signal potential transcription errors  
- soften the tone of unreviewed messages  
- document workflow for internal accountability  

DBNR was not a legal shield, but a social and procedural one.

---

# **3. Why the Notation Existed**
DBNR served several overlapping functions.

## **3.1 Acknowledgment of Delegation**
It made explicit that:
- the principal dictated the content  
- the secretary produced the text  
- the principal did not review the final version  

This parallels the attribution issues in **Paper 3**, where AI output is attributed to the user even without review.

## **3.2 Error Tolerance**
Recipients understood that:
- minor errors were possible  
- tone might not reflect the principal’s style  
- phrasing might be rough or abrupt  

DBNR acted as a softening mechanism.

## **3.3 Workflow Documentation**
In offices with multiple assistants, DBNR indicated:
- who dictated  
- who typed  
- whether review occurred  

This is analogous to modern audit trails discussed in **Paper 4**.

## **3.4 Social Signaling**
DBNR conveyed:
- the sender was busy  
- the message was functional, not polished  
- urgency or volume justified delegation  

This social dimension is often overlooked but important.

---

# **4. How Recipients Interpreted DBNR**
Recipients treated DBNR as a contextual cue.

### **4.1 Reduced Formality**
A DBNR letter was understood as:
- less formal  
- less polished  
- more utilitarian  

### **4.2 Increased Forgiveness**
Errors were interpreted charitably.

### **4.3 Tone Adjustment**
Recipients mentally adjusted tone, knowing the principal had not reviewed the phrasing.

### **4.4 No Reduction in Authority**
Crucially, DBNR did **not** reduce the binding nature of the communication.

This aligns with the legal doctrines in **Papers 1 and 2**:  
**Delegation does not diminish responsibility.**

---

# **5. Legal Treatment of DBNR Communications**
Courts historically treated DBNR letters as:

- **fully binding**  
- **fully attributable to the principal**  
- **subject to the same contractual and evidentiary standards**  

DBNR did not function as a legal disclaimer.  
It was merely a procedural annotation.

This mirrors the modern treatment of AI‑generated communications in **Paper 3**.

---

# **6. Comparison to Modern Disclaimers**
DBNR has modern analogues:

### **6.1 “Sent from my phone, please excuse typos.”**
A contemporary softening mechanism acknowledging:
- haste  
- reduced review  
- potential errors  

### **6.2 Automated Email Footers**
Such as:
- “This is an automated message.”  
- “Do not reply.”  
- “Generated by system.”  

These disclaimers acknowledge delegation to a machine.

### **6.3 AI‑Assisted Drafting Disclosures**
Emerging norms include:
- “Drafted with AI assistance.”  
- “Generated by automated system.”  

These disclaimers serve the same social and procedural functions as DBNR.

---

# **7. AI as the New Stenographer**
AI systems now occupy the role once held by human stenographers:

- They transform rough input into polished text  
- They interpret incomplete instructions  
- They introduce errors and tone drift  
- They produce messages the principal may not review  

The parallels are striking:

| Historical Stenographer | Modern AI System |
|-------------------------|------------------|
| Mishears dictation | Misinterprets prompt |
| Transcription errors | Hallucinations |
| Tone drift | Over‑confident phrasing |
| DBNR notation | AI‑use disclaimers |
| Human intermediary | Machine intermediary |

This continuity reinforces the communication‑theory limits described in **Paper 5**.

---

# **8. Conclusion**
“Dictated but not read” was an early acknowledgment of the risks inherent in delegated communication. It recognized:

- the inevitability of misalignment  
- the social need for contextual cues  
- the procedural need for workflow documentation  
- the persistence of principal responsibility  

These same dynamics govern modern communication involving:
- human assistants  
- automated systems  
- AI models  

DBNR was not a relic—it was a precursor.  
It anticipated the legal, organizational, and theoretical issues explored throughout this series.
