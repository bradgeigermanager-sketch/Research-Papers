A Highly Decoupled, Compounding Tragedy of the Commons
The scenario describes a market in which independent brokers compete to market products whose defects are systematically underrepresented, while organizational purchasers are incentivized to acquire those products rather than to maximize their actual long-term value. Each participant responds to their own incentives, yet the combined effect is the progressive degradation of the market's ability to accurately assess product quality.
Brokers benefit from presenting products as more attractive than their underlying characteristics warrant. Purchasers, in turn, may benefit personally or professionally from completing acquisitions, even when those acquisitions do not serve their organizations' long-term interests. Because the rewards for both parties are tied more closely to transactions than to subsequent performance, neither necessarily bears the full cost of inaccurate representations or poor purchasing decisions.
The resulting information asymmetry distorts competition. Product defects become less visible, buyers become less capable of distinguishing genuine value from persuasive marketing, and honest disclosure may become a competitive disadvantage. As these conditions spread across independent market participants, the incentives favoring misrepresentation can become self-reinforcing without requiring explicit coordination or malicious intent.
The tragedy is that the market's shared informational resources—accurate product assessments, credible disclosures, reliable comparisons, and institutional trust—are progressively depleted by the very activities that depend upon them. The costs of this deterioration are distributed across purchasing organizations and other stakeholders, while the immediate benefits of successful transactions accrue disproportionately to individual brokers and acquisition-oriented decision-makers.
The process becomes compounding when poor acquisitions, distorted performance assessments, and weak accountability reinforce subsequent decisions. It becomes highly decoupled when the people influencing purchasing decisions are insulated from the financial, operational, or reputational consequences of product failure. Together, these characteristics can produce a self-reinforcing market failure in which individually advantageous behavior generates collectively inferior outcomes, undermines the reliability of market information, and progressively impairs the system's capacity to correct itself.
In essence, it is a collectively self-undermining market system in which misaligned incentives, strategic information distortion, and separation from downstream consequences allow private transaction-level gains to accumulate at the expense of shared informational integrity and long-term organizational value.
