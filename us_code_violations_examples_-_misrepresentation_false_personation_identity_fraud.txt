Below are concrete, real-world violation examples for the core U.S. Code misrepresentation, false personation, and identity fraud statutes, aligned directly with the explicit text of each law:

📌 18 U.S.C. § 1001 (False Statements to Federal Agencies)

This omnibus statute applies to nearly every interaction with the federal government, and these common scenarios all qualify as violations:

Lying to federal EPA inspectors during a site visit, falsely claiming your industrial facility has fully installed required pollution control equipment, when the systems are non-functional and you have been illegally discharging untreated waste.
Submitting falsified payroll records to the U.S. Department of Labor for a federal construction grant, inflating the number of hours your employees worked to fraudulently collect an extra $45,000 in taxpayer-funded reimbursement.
Deliberately omitting your prior federal felony conviction for fraud on a U.S. security clearance application, to hide a disqualifying criminal history and obtain access to classified national defense information.
Making a knowingly false statement to U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service agents during an investigation, denying you illegally hunted protected bald eagles on federal public lands.
Falsely claiming you never received prior overpayments from the Social Security Administration during a routine benefits review, to avoid being forced to return thousands of dollars in improperly distributed public funds.
📌 18 U.S.C. § 1028 (Identity Document Fraud)

These scenarios directly violate the statute’s explicit prohibitions on unauthorized production, transfer, and possession of fraudulent identity materials:

Operating a home-based counterfeit ID mill, producing over 200 fake state driver’s licenses and fake U.S. resident green cards for undocumented immigrants, and selling each document for $800 in cash.
Purchasing a database of 12,000 stolen full names, Social Security numbers, and credit report data from a dark web marketplace, with the intent to use the information to open fraudulent credit card accounts in other people’s names.
Hoarding 17 stolen U.S. passports that you stole from a postal service distribution center, planning to resell them to human smuggling networks for cross-border trafficking.
Manufacturing specialized custom plastic card printers, hologram stickers, and government ID template software, and selling these tools exclusively to known counterfeiters who use them to produce fake federal identification documents.
Possessing a stolen U.S. Secret Service agent badge that you purchased at an online auction, with the intent to use it to impersonate a federal law enforcement officer to scam local businesses.
📌 18 U.S.C. § 1028A (Aggravated Identity Theft)

All these scenarios trigger the statute’s mandatory consecutive 2-year prison enhancement, as they use a real person’s stolen identity to commit a separate underlying federal felony:

Using a stranger’s stolen Social Security number and date of birth to submit a fraudulent Paycheck Protection Program (PPP) loan application during the COVID-19 pandemic, stealing $120,000 in federal small business relief funds.
Using a stolen elderly person’s Medicare ID number to submit $75,000 in false medical billing claims to the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, defrauding the federal healthcare program.
Using a stolen minor child’s full name and personal information to create a fake social media profile, then using that fake account to distribute child sexual exploitation material in violation of federal child pornography laws.
Using a stolen gun store customer’s identity information to fill out a falsified ATF Form 4473, illegally purchasing a short-barreled rifle that you are legally prohibited from owning due to a prior domestic violence conviction.
Using a stolen veteran’s military service record to fraudulently apply for and receive $60,000 in veteran disability benefits that you are not legally eligible to collect.
📌 18 U.S.C. § 1546 (Immigration Misrepresentation & Personation)

These scenarios violate the statute’s rules governing U.S. immigration systems and border entry processes:

Impersonating your U.S. citizen twin sibling at a U.S. port of entry, using their valid passport to fraudulently enter the United States after you were previously deported for a prior felony conviction.
Submitting a completely fake marriage certificate to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, falsely claiming you are married to a U.S. citizen to fraudulently qualify for an expedited green card.
Selling 12 of your unused, legitimate student visas to foreign nationals who are not enrolled in any U.S. university, helping them illegally enter the United States under false pretenses.
Lying on your naturalization application, deliberately hiding that you previously worked as a paid foreign agent for a hostile foreign government, to fraudulently obtain U.S. citizenship.
Using a deceased U.S. citizen’s birth certificate and identity documents to apply for a U.S. passport, so you can travel internationally under a completely false legal identity.
📌 18 U.S.C. § 911 (False Personation of a U.S. Citizen)

These scenarios all violate the statute’s ban on fraudulent claims of U.S. citizenship to access restricted federal benefits:

A non-citizen falsely claiming to be a U.S. citizen on a federal voter registration form, then casting an illegal ballot in a federal congressional election.
A non-citizen lying about their U.S. citizenship status on a federal student aid application, fraudulently qualifying for $32,000 in Pell Grants that are legally restricted exclusively to U.S. citizens.
A non-citizen asserting they are a U.S. citizen during a routine ICE traffic stop, to avoid being identified as an undocumented immigrant and taken into deportation custody.
A non-citizen falsifying their citizenship status on a federal government job application, to obtain a position at the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs that legally requires U.S. citizenship as a mandatory hiring qualification.
