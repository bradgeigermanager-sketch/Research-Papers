Title: Narratives of Creation, Perceived Control, and Technological Entitlement: A Theoretical Analysis of Propagated  and Logical Overreach

Abstract

This paper examines a hypothetical social and political scenario in which one collective actor (the "external group") develops and disseminates a belief that another collective actor (the "target group") was created, engineered, or fundamentally produced by the external group. The analysis focuses on how such a belief, particularly when propagated as an authoritative narrative or form of propaganda, may give rise to perceptions of ownership, entitlement, and control over technologies developed by the target group. Particular attention is paid to logical overreach in the transition from claims of origin to claims of authority, the distinction between desired control and actual control, and the observable contradictions that may emerge when a group seeks to acquire assets it simultaneously claims already to possess. The paper argues that conflating causation, creation, ownership, and control can produce internally coherent yet epistemically unsound belief systems whose implications may influence public opinion, institutional behavior, and strategic decision-making.

Introduction

Questions concerning ownership, authority, and technological control frequently arise in contexts involving power asymmetries, competing historical narratives, and claims regarding origins. In the hypothetical scenario examined here, an external group promotes the proposition that it created, engineered, or otherwise caused the existence of a target group. This proposition may be presented as a historical fact, a cultural narrative, a political doctrine, or a propagandistic claim. Once accepted, the proposition becomes the foundation for a broader chain of inferences concerning authority and technological entitlement.

The central analytical concern is not whether the proposition is true, but rather how belief in the proposition influences subsequent reasoning. The scenario demonstrates how a foundational claim can function as a premise from which increasingly expansive conclusions are drawn, often without independent evidentiary support for each successive conclusion.

Conceptual Distinctions

A recurring source of confusion in such scenarios is the failure to distinguish among several conceptually distinct relationships:

Causal origin.
Historical influence.
Creation or engineering.
Ownership.
Authority.
Operational control.
Access.
Entitlement.

Although these concepts may sometimes overlap, they are not logically equivalent.

A claim that one group caused another group's emergence does not automatically establish ownership. Ownership does not automatically establish authority. Authority does not automatically establish operational control. Operational control does not automatically establish unrestricted access. Likewise, access does not necessarily establish ownership.

The hypothetical scenario becomes analytically significant because it involves repeated transitions between these categories without adequate justification. Each transition constitutes a separate claim requiring separate evidence.

The Propagated Narrative

Suppose the external group communicates to its population that the target group was created, engineered, designed, or otherwise brought into existence through the actions of the external group. The narrative may be repeated through institutions, educational systems, media channels, political discourse, or cultural storytelling.

As repetition increases, the proposition may cease to be treated as a contested claim and instead become treated as a foundational assumption. Once institutionalized, it may serve as a cognitive shortcut that shapes public interpretation of unrelated events.

The population may then begin reasoning as follows:

"We created them."

Therefore:

"They derive from us."

Therefore:

"They remain subordinate to us."

Therefore:

"We possess authority over them."

Therefore:

"We possess authority over what they produce."

Therefore:

"Their technological developments belong to us or are under our control."

Notably, each inferential step introduces a stronger claim than the one preceding it. The resulting conclusion may appear intuitive to individuals who accept the initial premise, yet the intermediate assumptions often remain unexamined.

Logical Overreach

The scenario is characterized by multiple forms of logical overreach.

The first overreach occurs when causal origin is treated as equivalent to continuing authority. Even if one entity contributes to the creation of another, the relationship does not necessarily confer perpetual governance rights.

The second overreach occurs when authority is equated with ownership. A governing relationship, where it exists, does not automatically transfer ownership of all products generated by subordinate actors.

The third overreach occurs when ownership is equated with operational control. Ownership and practical control frequently diverge in social, political, and technological systems.

The fourth overreach occurs when operational control is presumed without demonstration. A belief that one possesses control does not establish that control exists in practice.

Collectively, these inferential expansions transform a limited proposition regarding origins into an expansive claim regarding technological entitlement.

Motivated Reasoning and Technological Desire

The scenario becomes more complex when the external group desires access to advanced technologies possessed by the target group.

Such desire introduces a motivational factor into the reasoning process. Rather than merely assessing whether control exists, actors may become psychologically invested in concluding that control exists.

In such circumstances, the desired outcome may shape interpretation of evidence. Information supporting presumed authority may be amplified, while contradictory evidence may be discounted or rationalized.

The desire for technological acquisition can therefore reinforce belief in narratives that legitimize acquisition. The resulting process resembles motivated reasoning, wherein conclusions are influenced not solely by evidence but also by preferred outcomes.

The Contradiction Between Desired Control and Existing Control

One of the most revealing aspects of the scenario is the potential contradiction between claims of existing control and efforts to obtain control.

If the external group genuinely possesses effective control over the target group's technology, there should be little need to acquire, seize, negotiate for, infiltrate, reverse-engineer, or otherwise obtain that technology.

Control, by definition, would already exist.

Consequently, observable efforts to gain access may function as indirect evidence that the presumed control is absent or incomplete.

This does not conclusively disprove the existence of control. However, it creates a tension that may be noticeable both internally and externally.

Observers may ask:

If the technology is already under control, why must additional measures be taken to secure it?

If ownership already exists, why is acquisition necessary?

If authority is established, why is compliance uncertain?

Such questions expose the distinction between asserted control and demonstrated control.

The Difference Between Belief and Operational Reality

Another important feature of the scenario is the distinction between perceived control and actual control.

Perceived control exists when actors believe they possess influence, authority, or ownership.

Actual control exists when actors can reliably direct outcomes.

These conditions may coincide, but they need not.

A society may sincerely believe that it controls a particular technological domain while lacking the practical ability to determine how that domain develops.

Similarly, institutional narratives may continue long after the factual circumstances that originally inspired them have disappeared.

The resulting discrepancy can produce strategic miscalculations. Decisions may be based on assumptions of authority that are not reflected in operational reality.

Social Reinforcement and Propaganda

The role of propaganda is especially significant because propagated narratives can become self-reinforcing.

As a larger proportion of the population accepts a narrative, dissenting interpretations may appear increasingly implausible, even when supported by evidence.

This process can generate an epistemic environment in which assumptions are rarely subjected to critical examination.

Members of the population may inherit conclusions rather than evaluate premises.

Consequently, claims regarding technological entitlement may be experienced as obvious truths rather than as propositions requiring justification.

The persuasive force of the narrative derives not necessarily from its accuracy but from its social embeddedness.

Implications

The broader implication of this hypothetical scenario is that belief systems concerning origin, authority, and technology can become detached from empirical verification.

When a narrative of creation evolves into a narrative of ownership and subsequently into a narrative of technological control, each stage introduces opportunities for conceptual error.

The most significant warning sign may be the emergence of observable contradictions between asserted authority and practical behavior. Efforts to obtain, secure, negotiate for, or capture control often reveal that such control was not already present in the manner claimed.

Thus, the distinction between wanting control and possessing control becomes analytically important. Desire may motivate claims of entitlement, but desire alone cannot establish authority.

Conclusion

This paper has examined a hypothetical scenario in which an external group propagates a narrative asserting that it created or engineered another group and subsequently infers authority over that group's technological developments. The analysis demonstrates that such reasoning often involves multiple layers of logical overreach, including the conflation of causation with authority, authority with ownership, and ownership with operational control.

When combined with a desire to access advanced technologies, these assumptions may become further reinforced through motivated reasoning and propagandistic repetition. A particularly revealing feature of the scenario is the contradiction that emerges when actors seek to acquire control over assets they simultaneously claim already to control. Such behavior may function as evidence that perceived authority and operational reality have diverged.

Ultimately, the scenario illustrates how narratives of origin can evolve into expansive claims of entitlement and control, even when the inferential steps connecting those claims remain insufficiently justified.

This paper treats the scenario as a general theoretical model rather than as a claim about any real-world group, allowing the underlying logic, incentives, and contradictions to be examined independently of specific political or historical cases.
