A useful way to operationalize your framework is to treat mismatch with reality or history as a detection problem, rather than asking merely whether an analysis “sounds reasonable.”

Here are practical rules of thumb.

 ## Rules of thumb for detecting reality–analysis mismatch

 1. **Check the premises before checking the conclusion.**\
    If the conclusion depends on several factual claims, verify those claims independently. A logically consistent analysis can still be wrong if its premises are wrong.
2. **Ask, “What would have to be true for this plan to work?”**\
    Turn the plan into explicit assumptions. Then test each assumption against current evidence or historical records.
3. **Separate facts, inferences, assumptions, and predictions.**\
    Treat these as different categories:
     A warning sign is when an assumption gradually gets referred to as though it were a fact.
   - **Fact:** independently documented.
   - **Inference:** conclusion drawn from facts.
   - **Assumption:** accepted for purposes of analysis but not established.
   - **Prediction:** claim about a future or uncertain state.
4. **Look for claims that have lost their provenance.**\
    Ask: _Where did this fact come from? When was it established? Has it changed?_\
    Information without an identifiable source, date, or evidentiary basis deserves additional scrutiny.
5. **Check dates aggressively.**\
    A surprisingly large number of planning failures come from information that was once correct but is no longer current. Ask:
   - Was this true at the relevant time?
   - Is it still true?
   - Was the source published before or after the event?
   - Is the analysis accidentally using future knowledge when reconstructing a historical decision?
6. **Compare predictions with what actually happened.**\
    For historical or completed decisions, perform a simple countercheck:\

   $$
   \text{stated expectation} \quad vs. \quad \text{observed outcome}.
   $$
   \
    Repeated divergence is evidence that the underlying model or assumptions need examination.
7. **Search for disconfirming evidence, not just supporting evidence.**\
    Instead of asking _“Can I find evidence for this?”_, ask:
    > “What evidence would make this analysis wrong?”
    Failure to identify plausible disconfirming evidence is itself a useful warning signal.
8. **Check whether excluded alternatives were actually considered.**\
    A plan can look comprehensive while having been generated from a prematurely narrowed choice set. Ask:
    > “What plausible alternative explanations, plans, or historical interpretations were excluded, and why?”
9. **Watch for circular verification.**\
    If the same model, dataset, source family, or assumptions generated both the original conclusion and its “validation,” the validation may not be independent.
    $$
   A \rightarrow C \rightarrow \text{validation of } C
   $$
    is much weaker than:
    $$
   A \rightarrow C
      \qquad
      B_{\text{independent}} \rightarrow \text{validation}.
   $$
10. **Look for unusually smooth narratives.**\
     Real historical and operational situations often contain contradictions, uncertainty, abandoned alternatives, unexpected events, and incomplete information. An account in which every fact neatly supports one conclusion deserves examination.
11. **Ask what information would have been available at the time.**\
     Historical analysis can become distorted through hindsight. Distinguish:\

    $$
    \text{what was knowable then}
    $$
    \
     from\

    $$
    \text{what we know now}.
    $$
12. **Check whether the plan is using its own consequences as evidence.**\
     If a system recommends an action, the action changes the environment, and the resulting state is then treated as confirmation of the recommendation, there is a potential feedback problem.
     $$
    \text{prediction}\rightarrow\text{action}\rightarrow\text{changed reality}
    $$
     The resulting observation is not necessarily independent evidence.
13. **Look for claims that remain unchanged despite contradictory evidence.**\
     A robust analysis should specify what evidence would cause revision. If new evidence repeatedly gets classified as irrelevant without a principled reason, the analysis may have become self-sealing.
14. **Check quantitative claims against independent quantities.**\
     Totals, dates, rates, populations, capacities, timelines, and resource requirements should often be independently calculable. Basic arithmetic or dimensional inconsistencies can expose much deeper planning errors.
15. **Test dependencies rather than individual steps.**\
     A plan may contain individually plausible steps while having an impossible dependency structure:\

    $$
    A \rightarrow B \rightarrow C
    $$
    \
     where B cannot actually occur before C, for example.
16. **Look for missing prerequisites.**\
     Ask:
     > “What must already exist for this step to happen?”
     Then recursively check those prerequisites. This is particularly useful for LLM-generated plans.
17. **Distinguish linguistic confidence from evidentiary confidence.**\
     A detailed explanation, citation-like language, numerical precision, or confident prose is not evidence that the underlying proposition is true.
18. **Check whether different sources are genuinely independent.**\
     Ten websites repeating the same underlying claim may represent one source rather than ten independent confirmations.
19. **Track information decay.**\
     Store important claims with:
      This makes it possible to identify stale information before it enters a new plan.
    - source,
    - timestamp,
    - relevant jurisdiction/context,
    - confidence,
    - conditions under which the claim becomes obsolete.
20. **Ask what would happen if the central assumption were false.**\
     A particularly useful test is:
     > “If our most important assumption is wrong, which parts of this plan fail?”
     Plans that have no contingency for their highest-impact assumptions are especially fragile.

 ## A compact diagnostic

 For an existing analysis or plan, I would use six questions:

 | Question | Warning sign |
| --- | --- |
| **Is it true?** | Unverified factual premises |
| **Was it true at the relevant time?** | Stale or anachronistic information |
| **How do we know?** | Missing or circular provenance |
| **What could falsify it?** | No identifiable disconfirming evidence |
| **What depends on it?** | Large downstream dependency |
| **What happened after it was acted upon?** | Feedback being mistaken for independent confirmation |

This produces a useful distinction between **information integrity** and **plan integrity**.

 ### Information integrity

 $$
\text{Source}
\rightarrow
\text{claim}
\rightarrow
\text{verification}
\rightarrow
\text{current/historical validity}
$$

 ### Plan integrity

 $$
\text{validated facts}
\rightarrow
\text{assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{constraints}
\rightarrow
\text{plan}
\rightarrow
\text{execution}
$$

 A system can pass one and fail the other. For example, all the factual inputs can be accurate while the resulting plan violates an operational constraint.

 ## An especially useful rule for AI-assisted systems

 I would add one overarching rule to your paper:

 > **The more consequential a claim becomes downstream, the less acceptable it is for that claim to depend solely on an upstream AI-generated assertion.**

 In other words, verification requirements should increase with **dependency and consequence**, not merely with how uncertain the model appears.

 That gives you a practical principle:

 $$
\text{Required verification}
\propto
\text{consequence}
\times
\text{dependency}
\times
\text{irreversibility}.
$$

This is particularly relevant to your “epistemic bottleneck” concept: the most important claims are precisely those that should have the **greatest independence of verification**, because an error there can propagate through the greatest portion of the system.

Counterfactual data introduces a somewhat different problem. The issue is not necessarily that the information is inaccurate. 
It may be internally accurate _within the hypothetical world_, while being mistakenly treated as evidence about the actual world.

A useful distinction is:

 $$
\text{counterfactual validity} \neq \text{empirical validity}.
$$

 For example, “If event X had not occurred, outcome Y would probably have followed” can be a perfectly legitimate counterfactual claim. The problem arises when that proposition subsequently gets stored, retrieved, planned around, or acted upon as though **X did not occur and Y actually happened**.

 ## Rules of thumb for detecting counterfactual–reality confusion

 1. **Label the ontological status of every important claim.**
    A useful taxonomy is:
     A major warning sign is movement between categories without an explicit transition.
    $$
   \text{counterfactual}
      \not\Rightarrow
      \text{historical fact}
   $$
   - **Observed:** directly established to have occurred.
   - **Recorded:** documented by a source.
   - **Inferred:** derived from observed information.
   - **Counterfactual:** describes what might have happened under different conditions.
   - **Simulated:** generated by a model under specified assumptions.
   - **Projected:** expected future state.
   - **Hypothetical:** deliberately constructed scenario.
2. **Ask, “Did this happen, or did the analysis say it would have happened?”**
    This is perhaps the simplest diagnostic.
    Compare:
    > “Under scenario X, the model estimates that outcome Y would occur.”
    with:
    > “Outcome Y occurred.”
    The second statement does not follow from the first.
3. **Preserve the counterfactual condition.**
    A counterfactual proposition normally has a condition:
    $$
   X \text{ had been different}
      \rightarrow
      Y \text{ might have occurred}.
   $$
    If the condition disappears during storage or summarization, the proposition can become dangerously misleading:
    $$
   Y \text{ might have occurred}
      \rightarrow
      Y \text{ occurred}.
   $$
    This is especially relevant to LLM-generated summaries, because compression can remove precisely the qualifiers that preserve the distinction.
4. **Store the world-state alongside the claim.**
    Instead of storing:
    > “Plan B would have prevented the failure.”
    store something closer to:
    > “Counterfactual claim: assuming conditions C1–C4, analysis estimated that Plan B would have reduced probability of failure.”
    The assumptions are part of the information.
5. **Ask whether the proposition has an actual-world referent.**
    For every consequential claim:
    > “What observation in the actual world would correspond to this statement?”
    If there is no such observation because the claim concerns an unrealized alternative, it should not be treated as an observation.
6. **Do not use unrealized outcomes as ordinary historical evidence.**
    Suppose:
    $$
   A \rightarrow B
   $$
    actually happened.
    A model says:
    $$
   \neg A \rightarrow C.
   $$
    C is not historical evidence about what actually happened after A. It is evidence about a hypothesized alternative trajectory.
    This distinction becomes especially important in historical reconstruction.
7. **Watch for counterfactual contamination of training or retrieval data.**
    A dataset may contain statements such as:
     These can be legitimate analytical records. But if they enter a corpus without their scenario labels, a retrieval system can later surface them as though they described actual events.
    This creates a form of **semantic data contamination without factual falsity**.
   - “Had the policy been implemented, unemployment would have fallen.”
   - “If the attack had occurred, the response would have been X.”
   - “Under Scenario B, demand reaches 20% higher.”
8. **Check whether the counterfactual was ever executed.**
    This is particularly important for planning.
    $$
   \text{proposed plan}
      \rightarrow
      \text{simulated outcome}
   $$
    does not establish:
    $$
   \text{proposed plan}
      \rightarrow
      \text{actual outcome}.
   $$
    If the plan was never implemented, its predicted outcome remains unvalidated.
9. **Separate counterfactual prediction from counterfactual explanation.**
    These are different claims.
    **Counterfactual prediction:**
    > “If X had occurred, Y would probably have occurred.”
    **Counterfactual explanation:**
    > “Because X did not occur, the absence of Y can be explained by X.”
    The second is substantially stronger and requires additional assumptions.
10. **Look for “counterfactual drift.”**

 This could be a useful term for your paper.

 A proposition can move through a system:

 $$
H
   \rightarrow
   H_{\text{simulated}}
   \rightarrow
   H_{\text{summarized}}
   \rightarrow
   H_{\text{stored}}
   \rightarrow
   H_{\text{retrieved}}
   \rightarrow
   H_{\text{planned}}
$$

 while progressively losing the label that it was hypothetical.

 By the end, the system may be acting on a proposition whose **epistemic status has silently changed** even though none of the underlying words are technically false.

 ## The particularly important distinction: false data versus fictional-world data

 This gives you an interesting extension of your original framework.

 With inaccurate information:

 $$
\text{false representation of actual world}
\rightarrow
\text{bad planning}.
$$

 With counterfactual contamination:

 $$
\text{accurate representation of hypothetical world}
\rightarrow
\text{mistaken representation of actual world}
\rightarrow
\text{bad planning}.
$$

 The latter is more subtle because a fact-checker may not find anything obviously false in the source.

 The statement may be perfectly accurate **conditional on the counterfactual assumptions**.

 The failure is a failure of **world-state attribution**.

 ## Counterfactuals can also create feedback loops

 This becomes particularly interesting in your cascading-error framework.

 Imagine:

 $$
C_0
\rightarrow
\text{counterfactual model}
\rightarrow
\text{plan}
\rightarrow
\text{action}
\rightarrow
C_1.
$$

 The system initially models an alternative world. It then acts based on that model, thereby changing the actual world.

 Now the distinction between:

 - what was predicted,
- what was assumed,
- what actually occurred,
- and what occurred because of the prediction

 becomes extremely important.

 You could call this **counterfactual-to-operational conversion**.

 The dangerous transition is:

 $$
\boxed{
\text{“If X, then Y”}
\rightarrow
\text{“Y is the expected state”}
\rightarrow
\text{“Act as though Y is the state”}
}
$$

 The final step converts a hypothetical proposition into an operational premise.

 ## A useful audit question

 For any piece of information entering an AI-assisted planning system, ask:

 > **“What world does this statement describe?”**

 Then classify it as:

 $$
W \in
\{
W_{\text{actual}},
W_{\text{historical}},
W_{\text{counterfactual}},
W_{\text{simulated}},
W_{\text{projected}},
W_{\text{hypothetical}}
\}.
$$

 The system should preserve that world-state designation whenever the information is stored, retrieved, summarized, or used for planning.

 This is analogous to **data provenance**, but it adds another dimension:

 $$
\text{provenance}
+
\text{world-state provenance}.
$$

 In other words, don't just ask **“Where did this information come from?”**

 Also ask:

 > **“Which world was this information true about?”**

That distinction could substantially strengthen your paper, because it broadens the problem from _inaccurate information_ to epistemic-state corruption: the system may possess information that is locally valid, but associate it with the wrong possible world, time, scenario, or state of affairs.

Incomplete data is arguably a distinct failure mode from both inaccurate data and counterfactual data.
The information available to the system can be entirely accurate, while the resulting analysis is still misleading because important portions of the relevant state are absent.

The key distinction is:

 $$
\text{accurate subset of information}
\neq
\text{accurate representation of the situation}.
$$

 In your framework, this is especially important because incompleteness can be **structural**: the missing information may systematically favor one interpretation or plan.

 ## Rules of thumb for detecting incomplete-data mismatch

 1. **Ask “What is missing?” before asking “Is what we have correct?”**
    Conventional verification asks whether the available facts are accurate.
    For incomplete-data problems, the more important question is:
    > What relevant facts are not represented here?
    An analysis can pass every factual accuracy check and still fail this test.
2. **Define the universe of relevant information.**
    Let:
    $$
   X_R = \text{relevant information}
   $$
    and:
    $$
   X_A = \text{available information}.
   $$
    Then:
    $$
   X_A \subset X_R
   $$
    represents an information gap.
    The important question becomes not merely how accurate $X_A$ is, but whether the missing portion $X_R-X_A$ could materially change the conclusion.
3. **Look for asymmetric missingness.**
    Missing data is particularly dangerous when it is not random.
    For example, suppose information is systematically missing about:
     The remaining dataset can be completely accurate while producing a systematically distorted picture.
   - unsuccessful alternatives;
   - negative outcomes;
   - dissenting assessments;
   - failed experiments;
   - resource constraints;
   - adverse cases;
   - affected populations;
   - historical exceptions.
4. **Ask whether the missing information is decision-relevant.**
    Not every missing fact matters.
    A useful question is:
    > “What missing information, if discovered, could change the decision?”
    This distinguishes ordinary incompleteness from **decision-critical incompleteness**.
5. **Test the conclusion against plausible missing information.**
    Instead of asking:
    > “Does the available evidence support the conclusion?”
    ask:
    > “Could a reasonable amount of currently missing evidence reverse, weaken, or substantially qualify the conclusion?”
    If yes, the conclusion should probably be conditional rather than categorical.
6. **Distinguish absence of evidence from evidence of absence.**
    This is one of the most important rules:
    $$
   \neg\text{observed}(X)
      \neq
      \neg X.
   $$
    If a database contains no record of an event, that does not necessarily establish that the event did not occur.
    Likewise:
    > “No risk was identified”
    does not necessarily mean:
    > “No risk exists.”
7. **Check coverage, not just correctness.**
    You can think of information quality as having at least two dimensions:
    $$
   Q = f(\text{accuracy},\text{coverage}).
   $$
    An information source can have extremely high accuracy but poor coverage.
    This is particularly important for retrieval systems, where the model may confidently synthesize only the documents that happened to be retrieved.
8. **Ask what determines what gets observed.**
    This is crucial when missingness is systematic.
    Ask:
    > Who collected the information?
    > What did they choose to measure?
    > What did they have access to?
    > What was excluded?
    > What could not be observed?
    Missingness itself can contain information about the process that generated the dataset.
9. **Check for survivorship bias.**
    If the dataset contains only successful cases, surviving entities, completed projects, responding participants, or published studies, the missing cases may be precisely the cases needed to understand failure.
    A planning system that learns only from visible successes can generate systematically optimistic plans without containing a single false datum.
10. **Check for selection effects.**
     Ask whether the information entering the system is conditional on some prior process:
     $$
    X \mid S
    $$
     where $S$ represents selection into the dataset.
     If so, conclusions about the selected sample may not apply to the broader population.
11. **Look for denominator problems.**
     Incomplete data can make rates appear meaningful when the denominator is unknown.
     For example:
     $$
    \frac{\text{observed failures}}
             {\text{observed cases}}
    $$
     may not represent:
     $$
    \frac{\text{all failures}}
             {\text{all relevant cases}}.
    $$
     A model can perform perfectly correct arithmetic on an inappropriate denominator.
12. **Check temporal coverage.**
     Information can be incomplete because an important period is missing.
     Ask:
      This matters particularly for historical analysis.
    - Are earlier conditions represented?
    - Are later developments represented?
    - Is there a gap around the event being analyzed?
    - Are we extrapolating from a short observation window?
13. **Check geographic, demographic, or organizational coverage.**
     A dataset can be comprehensive in one dimension and incomplete in another.
     For example:
     $$
    \text{complete records for A}
        \neq
        \text{representative records of A+B+C}.
    $$
     Claims about the whole system should therefore be checked against the population actually represented.
14. **Look for missing negative cases.**
     This deserves special attention in AI-generated analysis.
     A model may retrieve many documents explaining why something worked but fail to retrieve:
      The resulting synthesis can be factually accurate while systematically optimistic.
    - failed attempts;
    - abandoned projects;
    - contradictory evidence;
    - unsuccessful interventions;
    - warnings that proved correct.
15. **Ask what information the model would need to change its mind.**
     This is a powerful diagnostic:
     > “What additional information would cause you to choose a different plan?”
     If the answer is “nothing,” the analysis may have become insensitive to missing information.
16. **Distinguish unknown from unlikely.**
     Incomplete data often creates unjustified probability judgments.
     These are different:
     $$
    P(X)\text{ is low}
    $$
     and:
     $$
    P(X)\text{ is unknown}.
    $$
     Lack of evidence may justify uncertainty rather than a low probability.
17. **Track missingness through the planning chain.**
     Missing information can become more dangerous as it propagates:
     $$
    \text{missing data}
        \rightarrow
        \text{unstated assumption}
        \rightarrow
        \text{model inference}
        \rightarrow
        \text{plan}
        \rightarrow
        \text{action}.
    $$
     By the planning stage, nobody may remember that the original premise was simply unknown.

 ## The particularly dangerous phenomenon: assumption substitution

 I think this is a useful addition to your paper.

 When data is missing, a system often cannot stop reasoning. It fills the gap with something.

 That something might be:

 - an assumption;
- a default;
- a prior;
- an extrapolation;
- a learned pattern;
- a user-supplied belief;
- or an LLM-generated inference.

 So the transition can be:

 $$
\boxed{
\text{missing information}
\rightarrow
\text{implicit assumption}
\rightarrow
\text{treated as premise}
}
$$

 This is different from hallucination.

 The model doesn't necessarily invent a false fact and present it as true. It may simply **fail to preserve the distinction between “unknown” and “assumed for purposes of analysis.”**

 That distinction can disappear during planning.

 ## Incomplete data can interact with your counterfactual problem

 These two failure modes can combine:

 $$
\text{incomplete actual data}
\rightarrow
\text{counterfactual assumptions}
\rightarrow
\text{simulated outcome}
\rightarrow
\text{operational plan}.
$$

 For example, if the actual historical record is incomplete, a model may fill the gaps with a hypothetical reconstruction. If that reconstruction is subsequently stored without its hypothetical status, future systems may treat the reconstruction as historical evidence.

 So you can get:

 $$
\text{missing reality}
\rightarrow
\text{constructed possibility}
\rightarrow
\text{stored representation}
\rightarrow
\text{mistaken reality}.
$$

 That's a particularly interesting form of **epistemic laundering**: uncertainty or absence gets transformed into a seemingly concrete informational object as it moves through the system.

 ## A three-way distinction for your framework

 I would therefore distinguish three different data failures:

 | Condition | Core problem | Typical transformation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Inaccurate data** | What is represented is wrong | False → accepted as true |
| **Counterfactual data** | What is represented belongs to another possible world | Hypothetical → treated as actual |
| **Incomplete data** | Relevant reality is not represented | Unknown → implicitly assumed |

And they can interact:

 $$
\boxed{
\text{inaccurate}
+
\text{counterfactual}
+
\text{incomplete}
\rightarrow
\text{compound epistemic distortion}
}
$$

 ## One overarching test

 For your proposed system, I would formulate a general diagnostic around **three questions**:

 > **Is the information true?**

 > **Is it about the actual world being analyzed?**

 > **Is enough of the relevant world represented to justify the conclusion?**

 Those correspond roughly to:

 $$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Accuracy} &&\rightarrow&& \text{Is it true?}\\
&\text{World-state} &&\rightarrow&& \text{Which world/time/scenario does it describe?}\\
&\text{Coverage} &&\rightarrow&& \text{What relevant reality is missing?}
\end{aligned}
$$

 That gives you a broader framework than simply “hallucination” or “inaccurate information.” The deeper issue becomes **whether the information state used by the planning system is an adequate representation of the world to which the plan will actually be applied**.
