The Expectations Layer (Fourth Layer of the Unified Model)
### *How multi‑actor CI/HUMINT environments generate, propagate, and collide over predictions of the future*

The Expectations Layer models how each actor projects **future trajectories** off the same event \(P\).  
It sits *above* the Interpretation Layer and *feeds into* the Response Layer.

This layer captures the collapse of **predictive exclusivity**:  
> *No actor controls how the future is mentally priced in by others.*

---

# **1. Core Components of the Expectations Layer**

## **1.1 Event Node \(P\)**  
Same central event as in the other graphs: a crime, anomaly, operation, rumor, or threat.

## **1.2 Actor Nodes \(X\)**  
Each actor in the CI/HUMINT ecosystem forms expectations:

- Handler \(H\)  
- Confidential Informant / Source \(S\)  
- Offender / Target \(O\)  
- Rival agencies \(R_a\)  
- Supervisors / Command \(C\)  
- Prosecutors \(P_r\)  
- Media/Public \(M\)

Each actor has:

- **Information set** \(I_X\)  
- **Internal parameters** \(\psi_X\) (risk tolerance, incentives, priors)  
- **Predictive map**  
  \[
  \Pi_X(P) = g_X(P, I_X, \psi_X)
  \]

## **1.3 Expectation Nodes \(E_X\)**  
For each actor \(X\), create a node:

\[
E_X = \Pi_X(P)
\]

This is the actor’s **forecast of the future**, not just a belief about the present.

Examples:

- Handler: “This CI will stay loyal if managed.”  
- CI: “The handler will protect me even if I shade the truth.”  
- Offender: “Enforcement will stay fragmented; I can exploit it.”  
- Rival agency: “This case will blow up and embarrass them.”  
- Supervisor: “This operation will become a political risk.”  
- Media: “This story will escalate into a scandal.”

---

# **2. Edge Types in the Expectations Layer**

## **2.1 Information Edges**  
- \(P \rightarrow X\): actor becomes aware of the event.  
- \(X \rightarrow E_X\): actor generates its expectation.

## **2.2 Expectation‑Influence Edges**  
- \(E_X \rightarrow E_Y\):  
  One actor’s forecast influences another’s.

Examples:

- Handler’s expectations shape supervisor expectations.  
- Media expectations shape public and political expectations.  
- Rival agency expectations shape handler risk calculus.  
- Offender expectations shape CI expectations (fear of retaliation).

## **2.3 Action Feedback Edges**  
Expectations drive actions:

- \(E_X \rightarrow A_X\): actor acts based on its forecast.  
- \(A_X \rightarrow P'\): actions change the environment.  
- \(P' \rightarrow E_Y\): new state updates others’ expectations.

This creates **recursive futures**.

## **2.4 Conflict / Alignment Edges**  
Between expectation nodes:

- aligned  
- divergent  
- adversarial  

Examples:

- Handler vs CI: aligned on short‑term goals, divergent on long‑term risk.  
- Agency vs agency: adversarial expectations about credit or failure.  
- Media vs institution: adversarial expectations about scandal potential.

# **3. Structural Patterns in the Expectations Layer**

## **3.1 Expectation Clusters**

- **Institutional cluster:**  
  \(H, C, P_r, R_a\)  
  Often aligned around case success, low scandal.

- **Criminal cluster:**  
  \(S, O\)  
  Aligned around survival, betrayal risk, retaliation.

- **External cluster:**  
  \(M\), public  
  Aligned around narrative payoff, scandal potential.

## **3.2 Expectation Fault Lines**

Where clusters collide:

- Handler vs CI  
- Handler vs Offender  
- Agency vs Agency  
- Institution vs Media  

These are the **fault lines where futures diverge**.

## **3.3 Dominant Expectation Node \(D_E\)** (optional)

A node representing the **currently dominant future narrative**:

- “This will become a scandal.”  
- “This will quietly succeed.”  
- “This CI will flip.”  
- “This operation will collapse.”

Edges from \(E_X\) to \(D_E\) weighted by influence.

---

# **4. Dynamics of the Expectations Layer**

## **4.1 Expectation Updates**

As new information arrives:

\[
E_X^{(t+1)} = g_X(P^{(t+1)}, I_X^{(t+1)}, \psi_X)
\]

Expectations shift.

## **4.2 Expectation Cascades**

A shift in one influential \(E_X\) (e.g., media or a key supervisor) can trigger:

- rapid alignment  
- rapid divergence  
- panic  
- overreaction  
- paralysis  

## **4.3 Adversarial Expectation Manipulation**

Actors attempt to shape others’ futures:

- CI exaggerates threat to increase protection.  
- Handler downplays risk to avoid oversight.  
- Offender signals compliance to reduce enforcement expectations.  
- Rival agency leaks to shift expectations.

But per your lemma:  
**no one actor can monopolize the expectation field.**

## **4.4 Expectation Lock‑In**

Once a dominant expectation \(D_E\) stabilizes:

- actors conform to it  
- dissenting expectations become fringe  
- actions align with the dominant future  
- the system becomes path‑dependent

# **5. Expectation Graph Layout (parallel to your other graphs)**

- **Center:** \(P\)  
- **Inner ring:** actor nodes \(X\)  
- **Outer ring:** expectation nodes \(E_X\)  
- **Cross‑edges:** influence, conflict, alignment  
- **Optional:** dominant expectation node \(D_E\)

This mirrors the Observer and Interpretation Graphs, completing the trilogy.

---

# **6. How the Expectations Layer integrates with CI/HUMINT**

This layer explains:

- why handlers cannot control how risk is perceived  
- why CIs cannot control how their fabrications shape the future  
- why offenders cannot control how enforcement will react  
- why agencies cannot control how media will frame future outcomes  
- why supervisors cannot control how rival agencies will anticipate moves

**Everyone is projecting futures off the same \(P\), and those futures collide.**

This is the collapse of **predictive exclusivity**.
